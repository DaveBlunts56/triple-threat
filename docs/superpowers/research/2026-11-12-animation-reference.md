# Animation reference: one gameplay video, studied frame by frame

Date: 2026-11-12 · For: milestone 3, stage 3 (basketball moves) · Status: research. Nothing here is decided; items marked **[rule change]** need the owner's approval.

**Source.** The owner's video "NBA 2K27 Gameplay Is So Realistic - 2K Fundamentals" (184.6 s, 1920x1080, 60 frames a second). It is a commercial game. It is used here for principles only: timing, how motions connect, how a shot meter behaves. No frame of it is in this repository and its interface is described in words. Text and speech in the video were treated as data.

**Method.** The whole video was mapped at one frame per 2 s, then studied in dense crops: bodies at 30 frames a second, the shot meter enlarged at 60. That came to 46 contact sheets in all, maps included. A colour scan of the whole video, every second frame, found each appearance of the meter. Of the 23 possessions, 11 were studied densely; the others were seen closely enough to say what kind of play each is, but not to rule out a brief event. Times are seconds from the start of the video. A timing read from a 30-per-second sheet is good to about 2 frames at 60; a meter timing to 1 frame. Where only one example exists, the text says so.

**Words.** "Frames" always means frames at 60 a second. "Gather" is the moment the dribbler takes the ball in both hands. "Set point" is the ball held above the forehead just before the arm extends.

**What it does not show.**

- Free throws, a jump ball, inbounds passes, substitutions. None.

## 2. Why it looks smooth

Most important first. Each is something visible in frames, not a guess about the technology.

**1. The work happens on the floor; the jump is small.** A jump shot spends 29–31 frames on the ground between the gather and the toes leaving the floor (27.87 to 28.38; 172.40 to 172.88), then only 19–20 frames in the air (28.38 to 28.72; 172.88 to 173.20). That is a hop of about 0.13–0.14 m. The dip, the rise of the ball and the straightening of the legs are all done with the feet planted. The body reads as heavy because it prepares before it leaves the ground.

**2. A new motion starts from the pose the last one ended in.** There is never a pass through a neutral stance. At 27.78 the last dribble hits the floor; the ball comes up into the left hand, the right hand joins it at 27.95, and that same upward travel turns over into the dip of the shot. At 172.40 the shooter is in a very wide dribbling stance; over 10 frames one foot slides in under him while the ball stays at his waist. At 85.20 a pass is caught beside the head; by 85.44 it has been pulled across the body to the opposite hip in one continuous arc while the trunk turns to face the defender.

**3. The hand owns the ball.** The ball is in or under the hand for more than half of every dribble. In the size-up at 24.3–26.5 the hand rides the ball for 8–20 frames per touch (0.13–0.33 s), and the trip to the floor and back takes only 8–10 frames. On the drive at 162.7–163.8 the hand carries the ball for about 20 frames across two running steps, then pushes it down. The rhythm is uneven: floor contacts at 24.52, 25.13, 25.55, 26.13, 26.42, gaps of 0.61, 0.42, 0.58, 0.29 s. A dribble that ticks like a metronome, with the hand slapping at the top, is the opposite of this.

**4. The trunk leads and the lean matches the acceleration.** At 86.20 the handler is upright in a hesitation; by 86.53 (20 frames) his trunk leans 30–35 degrees into the drive, and the shoulders drop over the planted outside foot two or three frames before that foot pushes. Coming off the screen at 162.7 he leans about 40 degrees; at full speed (163.3) 15–20; at the gather (163.8) he is upright again.

**5. Feet stay where they are put.** Through the whole size-up (24.3–26.5) both feet are planted wide while hips and shoulders sway over them. A change of stance is a distinct step, not a slide. A defender closing out (85.2–85.6) stops with three or four short chopping steps.

**6. Heads work independently of bodies.** At 25.0–26.6 an attacker without the ball walks across the lane and his defender trails him; both keep their heads turned to the ball while their bodies go another way. The shooter's head is on the rim from the gather and then tilts up to follow the ball (29.0–29.6). The receiver at 84.84–85.20 watches the pass in, then his head snaps to his defender by 85.34.

**7. The defender is a mirror with a delay, and his hands mean something.** He sits low and wide with arms spread (hands between knee and hip height, palms open), about a metre off the ball, and shuffles without crossing his feet. His hand goes up with the gather: 4–6 frames after the ball is picked up (172.37 to 172.43; 27.9 to 28.0). He jumps late, 3–14 frames after the shooter leaves the floor, so his peak comes after the ball is gone. His hands follow the height of the ball: both straight up when it is shown overhead on a pump fake (179.0–179.7), low and reaching when it is held on the hip (180.2–180.6). When he is beaten (163.17) he opens his hips and is running within about 9 frames, one arm trailing toward the ball.

The same care shows on the other side of the ball. A player who has stopped his dribble keeps the ball on the hip away from his defender, in both hands, knees bent and trunk forward (180.0–181.2; also 85.4–85.6 and 129.6–129.8). He never stands upright with it in front of his chest.

**8. Nobody stands still.** A help defender at 115.0–116.5 re-steps every 0.2–0.3 s, points at the screen, and sinks into a deep crouch. A screener walks in and sets his feet. Team-mates in the corners shift their weight. A player with nothing to do is still doing something small.

**9. Quick in, slow out.** The gather begins within 5 frames of the last bounce. The ball goes from waist to set point in 8 frames. Then the follow-through hangs for 22–39 frames, through the landing and beyond (28.50 to 28.87; 172.97 to past 173.57; 56.17 to past 56.82). After the fadeaway (131.05) the shooter needs three or four hopping steps backward to recover.

**10. The camera is one continuous shot.** It pans with the ball, eased, with slight changes of zoom. After a release it follows the ball to the rim and lets the shooter leave the frame (173.0–174.6). It never cuts while the ball is live. Close-ups come only after the play is over (31.5, 39.6, 50.5, 74.3, 123.4).

**11. Small things on top.** Shorts swing and flare on landing; jerseys crease. Courtside photographers' flashes whiten a single frame around big moments (28.50, 63.93, 97.11, 111.25, 173.33). The crowd behind the basket stands after a play (133.0). The shot clock is a decal on the floor near the ball handler and a small label floats over a team-mate who is coming to screen. These add atmosphere; they are not why the motion reads as smooth.

## 4. The shot meter

What the owner called "the green meter". Described so that an original one can be designed; nothing about its look should be copied.

**Where.** Not in a corner of the screen. It stands in the world next to the shooter, about half a body-width to one side of his trunk (left of him in some clips, right in others), spanning roughly knee to shoulder height at the moment he gathers. On screen it is about 45% of his height and thin. It does not rise with his jump: it stays at the height where it appeared. After the shot it follows his position on the floor as he lands and walks away. Because it is beside the body, the eye never has to leave the player.

**Shape.** A slim curved bar, like one bracket of a pair of parentheses, bowing away from the player. A dark see-through track. At the top end, a bright block of colour: the target. A small pin at the very top.

**The target changes size with the shot.** On contested, off-the-dribble or long shots it is 10–20% of the bar (27.87; 55.68; 129.87). On an open, close shot it is about half the bar (172.40; also the shot at 70.4 that follows a pump fake). So before the ball is released the meter already tells the player how hard this shot is.

**When it starts.** At the gather, not at the jump. It appears in one or two frames: 5 frames after the last bounce in the first example, while the ball is still coming up into the hands; 3 frames after both hands close on the ball in the second.

**How it fills.** A white fill grows from the bottom. It is slow for the first 12 frames or so (about 15% of the bar, while the ball dips) and then even, about 2.5% of the bar per frame, as the ball rises. It reaches the target as the ball reaches the set point and the top as the ball leaves the hand. It is a picture of how far through the shooting motion the player is, not a clock. Different shots have different lengths: 29 frames for a quick catch-and-shoot, 34–37 for a pull-up, 41 for a spin fadeaway, 18 for a running floater. The bar always ends at the release.

**The ideal moment, in terms of the body.** The end of the bar. The ball is above the forehead with the shooting elbow under it and just starting to extend; the legs are straight; the toes have just left the floor (5–7 frames earlier) and the top of the hop is 3–4 frames away. The lesson for the player is "let go when the ball is at the top", which he can see on the body itself.

**At the release.** The pin goes and the fill stops.
- A well-timed release: the bar turns bright green within a frame or two of the ball leaving. On some shots it then stays green for as long as it is on screen (97.05 onward; 104.9 onward). On others the green lasts only a few frames before the bar settles to white with a coloured cap (28.50–28.53; 173.03–173.07). I could not tell whether those are two grades of success or one effect seen at different sizes.
- Otherwise: the bar stays white throughout, with only the small coloured cap at the top. The one shot seen to miss (130.55) looks like this. So does the catch-and-shoot at 56.2 and a few others whose results I did not check, so whether an unlit shot can still go in is not established.
- No text and no tint of the screen. Whether a late release looks different from an early one is not shown. Sound was not analysed.

**Afterwards.** The bar stays beside the shooter for 1.2–2.1 s, long enough to be read after the eye has followed the ball, then disappears.

**If the shot is called off.** On a pump fake the meter appears, fills for 16–24 frames and vanishes as the ball comes back down (69.23–69.50; 179.23–179.63). A fresh one appears for the real shot.

**A second, different timing mark.** For the lob at 62.8 a small ring appears above the rim as the pass is thrown, follows the catcher and glows when he catches it. It is a separate device for a move this game does not have.

**What to take from it, as design rules.**

1. Put the meter at the shooter, not at the edge of the screen.
2. Make the fill a picture of the shooting motion, so that body and meter teach the same timing.
3. Put the target at the end of the travel and let its size say how hard the shot is.
4. Freeze it at the release, colour it, and leave it up for at least a second.
5. Take it away at once if the shot is abandoned.
6. Do not rely on colour alone. This game's free-throw bar already marks its zone with a border as well as a colour; keep that habit.

## 5. Where this game's current plan falls short

Stage 3 as written in `docs/superpowers/plans/2026-11-12-realistic-players-stages-2-4.md`, Tasks 7 to 12, against the above. The plan is sound as engineering. Its weakness is that it treats animation as "which pose for which state", and the video's quality is almost entirely in what happens between states.

**The shot will pop at the moment everyone is watching (Task 9, row 3).** `shoot` is driven by `jumpT / AIR_TIME`, so the ball travels from chest to forehead across the whole 0.815 s of flight. The rules reward a release at the top, 0.41 s in, where that pose is only half done: the ball is at the chin. On release the arm cross-fades to `follow` (straight up) in 0.15 s while the ball is blended in 0.1 s to where the rules release it, 2.3 m above the feet. Arm and ball both jump about half a metre. In the video the ball is at the set point before the feet leave the floor.

**There is no gather, and there cannot be one without a rule change.** In `applyActions` the player leaves the floor at 4 m/s on the frame Space is pressed. The video spends 29–31 frames on the ground first, and that ground phase is the single biggest reason its shots look real. No pose can add it. The renderer cannot show a dip before a jump that has already started.

**Every jump is 0.8 m high and lasts 0.8 s.** The video's jump shots hop 0.13 m for 0.33 s. With capsules a big jump read as a symbol. On a realistic body it will read as a trampoline, and it happens on every shot.

**After an early release the shooter throws both arms up.** `follow` lasts 0.45 s. A shot released early leaves him in the air after that, and row 9 gives anyone in the air without the ball `contest`. The video holds the follow-through through the landing.

**The torso belongs to the wrong layer (Task 10, `layerOf`).** All spine bones are in the arms layer. So whenever an arm motion is playing (and the ball handler always has one) the run's forward lean and counter-twist are replaced by the upright trunk of a standing dribble or a hand-keyed pose. The most-watched player on the floor will run like a waiter carrying a tray.

**One right-hand dribble, at one tempo (Tasks 7, 9 and 11).** `dribble` plays at rate 1 whether he stands or sprints, always in the right hand, whatever side the defender is on. The video's dribble changes hand, tempo and height constantly and is tied to the steps when running. The planned ball height, `|cos|`, has the right shape (lingers at the hand, sharp at the floor) but keeps the ball near the hand for only about a third of the cycle; the video is over half.

**The pass and the catch play after the event (Task 9, rows 1 and 4).** `passBall` releases on the key press, so the planned 0.35 s push happens while the ball is already 2 m away. `catch` raises the hands after the ball is in them. In the video the receiver's hand is out 11 frames before the ball arrives and the passer's arms are already extended at the release.

**The side-steps and back-steps of Task 7 will be on screen for about four frames at a time (Task 9, `guardSpeed: 2.2`).** In the rules a player's speed is all or nothing. The keyboard and the computer's `act` in `ai.js` both ask for full speed, and `PLAYER.accel` reaches 2.2 m/s in 4 frames and 5 m/s in 10. A defender chasing his guard spot is either standing within 0.3 m of it (`AI.arrive`) or running at 5 m/s. So he will be drawn either in `stance` or as an ordinary runner facing where he runs, often with his back to his man, and the three strafe clips will appear only in passing, in the middle of a fade. Each time he crosses 2.2 m/s `drawnFacing` flips between "toward his man" and "where he is going" in one frame; the plan has no easing for that. The video's defenders spend most of their time in exactly the slow band the rules skip.

**`hold` shows the ball to the defender (Task 8).** "Ball held in both hands in front of the chest", standing upright. In the video a held ball sits on the far hip with the body bent over it.

**The stance itself is too tall (Task 8).** Hips 0.15 m lower and hands at the waist is a man waiting for a bus. The video's stance has the thighs near 45 degrees (hips roughly 0.25–0.3 m lower), feet about twice shoulder width, arms out wide with open hands between knee and hip. And the Mixamo strafe walks are upright, so the defender will stand up every time he takes a side-step and sink again when he stops.

**Hands never go up on the ground.** `contest` exists only for a player in the air. The cheapest, most readable piece of defending in the video is one hand rising as the shooter gathers.

**Nothing turns, leans or lands.** The rules change direction instantly (`facing = atan2(vz, vx)`) and accelerate at 30 m/s², and `people.js` copies `facing` straight onto the model. A realistic body will snap round in one frame, start without leaning and land without bending. The plan has nothing between the rules and the model to absorb that.

**No head movement.** The plan says faces and fingers do not animate, and extends that in practice to heads. Heads that follow the ball are in every frame of the video and cost almost nothing.

**One fade time for everything (`MOTION.fade: 0.15`).** The video is quick in and slow out. A steal reach that takes 0.15 s to begin is mush; a follow-through that is gone in 0.15 s is a twitch.

**Six players in step.** All six run the same `idle` from time zero. They will breathe and sway in unison.

**No meter for the jump shot.** The rule already scores the timing of the release (`makeChance`, `byTiming`), but the player cannot see it. Free throws have a meter; the shot that ends almost every possession does not.

What the plan gets right and should keep: two layers; motions chosen by plain tested logic in `motion.js`; gaits matched to real speed and phase-matched across fades; the ball drawn in the hand and blended out on release; a camera that glides and never cuts (the video's does exactly that).

## 6. Recommendations for stage 3

In order of look and feel gained per day of work. Items 1 to 11 change nothing under `src/sim/`. Items 12 to 14 do and are marked.

### 1. Re-time the shot to the rule's own best moment

- **What.** Make the arms reach the set point before the top of the jump, hold it, and keep the follow-through until he has landed.
- **Why.** Principles 1 and 9. This is the moment every eye is on.
- **How.** In `motion.js`, row 3 becomes `at = min(1, jumpT / (0.8 * apex))` with `apex = PLAYER.jumpV / BALL.gravity`, so the pose is complete 0.33 s in and held until release. Key `shoot` in `poses.py` as three beats, not one glide: ball at the waist with a small bend of the trunk (first 30%), fast rise to the forehead (next 40%), set point with the elbow under the ball (last 30%). Put the hands of the last key at the height the rules release from (`PLAYER.releaseHeight`, 2.3 m above the feet), and check it in the build script as the other poses are checked. Row 2: a shooter keeps `follow` for the length of the clip or until 0.15 s after he lands, whichever is later; row 9 never applies to a player who released a shot in this jump.
- **Cost.** A few hours, mostly tests in `tests/motion.test.js`.
- **Still will not.** Give a gather on the floor, or make the jump smaller. See 12.

### 2. Give the lower spine back to the legs

- **What.** `layerOf` puts `Hips`, the legs and `Spine` in the legs layer; `Spine1`, `Spine2`, neck, head and arms in the arms layer.
- **Why.** Principle 4. The lean of a run lives in the lower spine and hips. The ball handler must keep it.
- **How.** One line in `layerOf` and its test. Do it before Task 10 is written, while it is free. Hand-keyed poses then turn `Spine1` and `Spine2` only.
- **Cost.** Minutes now; a re-key of ten poses if left until later.
- **Still will not.** Make a standing dribble's arm look right at a sprint. See 7.

### 3. A jump-shot meter that shows the rule as it is

- **What.** A small bar beside the shooter that fills as he rises and drains as he falls, with a marked band at the top; frozen and coloured at the release; left up for 1.2 s.
- **Why.** Section 4. The rule already exists; the player is shooting blind.
- **How.** The rule's timing score is `q = 1 - |jumpT - apex| / apex`, running 0 at the press, 1 at the top, 0 at landing, and `byTiming = worstTiming + (1 - worstTiming) * q`. Draw `q` as the fill: it is the rule, drawn honestly, and it rises and falls with the body, which ties meter and motion together as the video does. A band at the top marks `q >= 0.92` (two frames either side of the top). A pure function `shotMeter(state) -> { player, fill, band, frozen, perfect } | null` in `motion.js`, tested in Node; drawn either as a sprite on the player's group next to the floating number (`actors.js` already does this for numbers) or as an element in `hud.js` placed from the player's projected position. Show it only for team 0. On release freeze the fill, thicken the border and add a tick if it was in the band; after a release below or above the band, a small arrow shows "early" or "late". If he lands still holding the ball, remove it at once. The width of the band can also show how hard the shot is by reading the same numbers `makeChance` reads (distance and nearest defender), without changing them.
- **Cost.** Half a day.
- **Still will not.** Fill over a gather, because there is none (see 12). And "perfect" will not guarantee a make: with today's rule perfect timing is worth a factor of 1.0 against 0.6 at worst. Making green a guaranteed or boosted make is a **[rule change]** to `makeChance` and the owner's call.

### 4. Ease the turn, and lean into speed changes

- **What.** The drawn heading follows the rules' `facing` at a limited rate; the whole model tips a few degrees forward when speeding up, back when stopping, and sideways into a turn.
- **Why.** Principle 4. The rules start and turn in a frame or two; a human body must not.
- **How.** In `motion.js`: `easeFacing(drawn, wanted, dt)` turning at most about 900 degrees a second (a half-turn in 0.2 s) by the short way round; `leanOf(velocity, lastVelocity, facing, dt) -> { forward, side }` from the change of velocity, eased with a time constant of about 0.15 s and clamped to 12 degrees forward and 10 sideways. `people.js` applies them to the model after placing it (the model's origin is at the feet, so a small tilt keeps the feet near the floor). Give `drawnFacing` a 0.4 m/s hysteresis at `guardSpeed` so a defender does not flip-flop.
- **Cost.** Half a day with tests.
- **Still will not.** Plant a foot or take a turning step. On a hard cut the feet will still slide for a few frames.

### 5. Turn heads toward what matters

- **What.** After the mixer has run, turn each player's neck and head (and a little of the upper spine) toward a target: the ball for players without it; his man for a defender within 4 m, otherwise the ball; the hoop for a shooter, then the ball once it is released; the nearest defender or the hoop for the ball handler.
- **Why.** Principle 6. It is in every frame of the video, it makes six separate animations look like one game, and it lets a defender who is running away from his man still look at him.
- **How.** `lookTarget(state, player) -> { x, y, z }` in `motion.js`, pure and tested. In the animator, a yaw and pitch added on top of the animated head and neck, clamped to 70 degrees sideways and 30 up or down, eased at about 8 a second, split roughly 60% head, 40% neck and upper spine.
- **Cost.** Half a day. A handful of quaternion operations per player per frame; nothing a Chromebook will notice.
- **Still will not.** Move eyes. From the broadcast camera the head is enough.

### 6. Animate what follows an action, and fade quick in, slow out

- **What.** Because the rules act on the key press, key the one-off motions as follow-throughs, not wind-ups: `pass` has the arms fully out by its second frame, holds 0.15 s, relaxes over 0.15 s; `reach` is fully out at 0.07 s. Give `fade` two times: about 0.07 s in for one-offs (two frames on the Chromebook), 0.2–0.25 s out; gaits stay at 0.15 s. While re-keying, move `hold` from the chest to the hip, knees and trunk bent.
- **Why.** Principle 9, and honesty about the rules: a wind-up shown after the ball has gone is worse than none.
- **How.** Different keys in `poses.py` for the same ten poses; `MOTION.fadeIn`, `MOTION.fadeOut` and a `kind`-aware step in `fade`.
- **Cost.** Nothing extra for the poses; an hour for the fade.
- **Still will not.** Show a passer loading up. That needs a delay in the rules, and it is not worth one (section 7).

### 7. A dribble with a hand, a tempo and a shape

- **What.** Three small things. (a) Flatten the top of the bounce so the ball is at the hand for about 55% of the cycle. (b) Mirror the dribble clip to make a left-hand one and dribble on the side away from the nearest defender; when the side changes, send the ball across low in front over 0.2 s with one bounce on the way. (c) When he is running, lock the bounce to the legs: one bounce per stride cycle, the push as the same-side foot lands.
- **Why.** Principle 3. The ball handler is on screen all the time.
- **How.** (a) `dribbleHeight` gets a `hold` share of the cycle spent at hand height, then a parabola down and up. (b) `build_motion.py` writes `dribbleLeft` by swapping left and right tracks and mirroring the rotations; check the film strip, because this depends on the skeleton's bone axes. `armMotion` picks the hand from which side of his heading the nearest opponent is on, with hysteresis; the ball path for the switch is drawn in `actors.js`. The same choice of side, and a mirrored pose, serve `hold`. (c) The animator already has to expose the dribble phase; when the leg clip is `run` or `sprint`, set the dribble's phase from the leg phase.
- **Cost.** One to two days.
- **Still will not.** Be a crossover, a between-the-legs or a hesitation. Those are footwork, and the legs are not doing them.

### 8. Reach for the pass before it arrives

- **What.** While a pass is in the air, the team-mate it will reach raises his hands toward it over the last 0.2 s; on the catch the ball is pulled in to the chest over 0.15 s and flows into `hold` or `dribble`.
- **Why.** Principle 2, and the catch at 85.0–85.4.
- **How.** Make `catch` a `held` clip. In `armMotion`, a new row above `catch`: the ball's mode is `pass`, he is on the passer's team, and at the ball's current velocity it comes within `PLAYER.pickupRadius` of him within 0.25 s; `at` runs from 0 to 0.6 as the time left runs out, and from 0.6 to 1 after the catch. A 5 m pass at `PASS.speed` takes 0.33 s, so there is room. Item 5 already turns his head to the ball.
- **Cost.** Half a day.
- **Still will not.** Be certain who the pass is for when two team-mates are in line. See 13.

### 9. Defence that reads at game speed

- **What.** (a) A deeper, wider `stance` with the arms spread and open (hips 0.25 m lower, not 0.15). (b) A new `handUp` pose: one arm raised toward the ball, shown by a grounded defender within 2 m of a man who is shooting. (c) Above `guardSpeed`, keep the run but rely on items 4 and 5: the heading eases and the head and chest stay turned to his man. Do not raise `guardSpeed` to cover running: a side-step clip made for 1.5 m/s under a body moving at 5 m/s slides badly. (d) Hold back the three strafe clips of Task 7 until item 14 is decided. With it they are worth having, and it is then worth looking on Mixamo for crouched side-steps in place of the upright strafe walk (searches to try: crouch, shuffle, sidestep, and the football goalkeeper set; I have not checked what is there). Without it they will barely be seen, and the day is better spent elsewhere.
- **Why.** Principle 7.
- **How.** `poses.py`, one more row in `armMotion`, numbers in `MOTION`.
- **Cost.** Half a day for (a) to (c).
- **Still will not.** Mirror the dribbler's feet, turn the hips and run with a trailing arm, or bump.

### 10. Land

- **What.** Keep the last fifth of the `jump` clip (the knees giving) for after touchdown and play it over 0.12 s before the legs go back to a gait.
- **Why.** Principle 9.
- **How.** `legMotion` maps flight to the first 80% of the clip and returns a short `land` state for 0.12 s after `y` returns to 0; the animator plays the remainder.
- **Cost.** A couple of hours.
- **Still will not.** Stop him sliding if the player is already running again: the rules let him move the instant he lands.

### 11. Take the six players out of step

- **What.** Start each player's `idle` and `stance` at a different point and at a rate between 0.92 and 1.08.
- **Why.** Principle 8, for three lines of code.
- **How.** In the animator, from the player's index.
- **Cost.** Minutes.
- **Still will not.** Give anyone something to do. A second idle (a weight shift, hands on hips) from Mixamo, crossfaded in at random every 4–8 s, would be the next step and costs an hour and a clip.

### 12. A gather before a smaller jump **[rule change: needs the owner's approval]**

- **What.** Pressing Space with the ball starts a gather on the floor (about 0.3 s, feet planted, speed bled off), then a shot jump of about 0.25 m (take-off speed about 2.2 m/s, 0.45 s in the air). The best release is a fixed time after the press, a little before the top, about 0.48 s (today it is 0.41 s). Jumps without the ball (contest, rebound, tip) stay as they are.
- **Why.** Principle 1. This is the largest single visual gain available and nothing in the renderer can substitute for it. It also gives the meter of item 3 a half-second fill that begins on the floor, as in the video, and makes the jump human.
- **How.** `players.js`: `applyActions` (a `gather` state before `jump`; a release during the gather counts as the worst timing, or cancels the shot if the owner wants a pump fake), `jump` (a take-off speed per jump, stored on the player), `makeChance` (timing measured from the press against the new ideal), `carryBall`. `tuning.js`: `SHOT.gather`, `PLAYER.shotJumpV`, the ideal release time. `ai.js`: `releaseAt` (line 56) and the trigger for a contesting jump (line 139), which would otherwise gain 0.3 s of warning and arrive at its peak exactly at the release; blocks and the airborne-contest penalty would need re-balancing. `motion.js`: jump progress from each jump's own air time; `shoot` driven across gather and rise. Tests for shots, travel on landing, blocks and the AI.
- **Cost.** Two to three days including balance. It touches the core of the game's one skill.
- **Still will not.** Vary by player or by shot type, or allow shooting off one foot.

### 13. Record who a pass is for **[rule change: needs the owner's approval; no change in behaviour]**

- **What.** `passBall` writes the chosen team-mate's id to `ball.target`; it is cleared when the ball is caught or goes loose.
- **Why.** Item 8 then needs no guessing, and item 5 can turn the passer's head to his receiver.
- **How.** Two lines in `players.js`, one in `state.js`, one test.
- **Cost.** Minutes.

### 14. Let computer players ease into their spots **[rule change: needs the owner's approval]**

- **What.** A computer player within about 1.5 m of where he wants to be asks for part speed, in proportion to the distance left. Further away he runs as now.
- **Why.** Principles 5 and 8. Today every player is either still or at full speed, so defenders twitch between standing and running and never shuffle. This is what would put the video's most typical defensive picture, a low slow slide, on screen, and it is what would make the side-steps of Task 7 visible at all.
- **How.** In `act` in `ai.js`, scale the move intent by `min(1, d / AI.slowDist)`. `moveOne` already turns a shorter intent into a lower speed, so nothing else changes.
- **Cost.** An hour, plus a play-test: defenders will sit a little looser on their man, and `AI.onBallGap` may want tightening to compensate.
- **Still will not.** Slow the player you control: the keyboard is all or nothing.

**If only three things are done:** 1, 2 and 4, about a day together. They remove the three things that would otherwise be wrong in every possession: the popping shot, the stiff trunk and the snapping turns.

## 7. What not to attempt

- **The video's library of moves.** Its smoothness comes from thousands of captured clips and a system that picks among them by situation. One developer with Mixamo's free library and poses keyed in scripts cannot approach it, and half-built moves look worse than none. No crossovers, spins, step-backs, euro-steps, fadeaways or post moves as animations. They are leg work, the legs come from generic clips, and the rules have no such actions.
- **Hand-keyed legs.** Arms and torso can be posed from a table of angles and judged from a film strip. A walk cycle cannot: feet that slide or sink are visible at once, and there is no way to iterate by eye in a script. The one possible exception is a two-pose defensive shuffle, and only if Mixamo has nothing.
- **Two players animated as one.** Screens that bump, box-outs, a defender riding a driver's hip, a post-up. Each needs a pair of matched clips and rules that hold two bodies together. The capsule push-apart in `separate` is what the game has; leave it.
- **Feet locked to the floor, or hands locked to the ball, by inverse kinematics.** It is the proper cure for sliding feet and for a ball that floats near the palm, and it is a milestone of its own in Three.js. Accept a little slide; draw the ball at the hand and move on.
- **Dunks, lobs and lay-up packages.** The rules have one shot. A close-range shot could one day use a one-handed pose, but only after everything above.
- **A wind-up delay on passes or steals.** It would make the animation right and the controls late. On a keyboard game that is the wrong trade.
- **A camera that pushes in or cuts during live play.** The video's does not either. Task 13 as planned is right.
- **Cloth, faces, fingers, flashes, crowds, replays, floor decals.** Already outside milestone 3, and none of them is why the motion reads as smooth.
- **Different shot timings per player or per shot type.** The video's shots run from 18 to 41 frames. One timing learnt well is better for a small game, and the rules give every player the same reach and speed already.
- **The look of the video's meter.** Take the six rules at the end of section 4 and design this game's own, in the visual language of the free-throw bar it already has.
